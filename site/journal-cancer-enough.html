Door Four · Cancer

Enough

Your Enough
Read

There's a race almost everyone is running, and no one remembers signing up for it.

More. Better. The next number. A finish line that keeps sliding back the second you get close, painted by someone who isn't you.

Nobody stops. Because stopping feels like losing. So they run their whole life at a line that was never real, and call the exhaustion ambition.

I once lived with someone who ran the whole race. All the way to the end.

When I moved to Florida at twenty-two, I moved in with my grandfather. My vovô. He was ninety-two. On his birthday, I sat and watched the sunset with him. Then I drove back, and the next morning I watched my sister give birth for the first time. An old life and a brand new one, one sunset apart.

Somewhere in there, vovô said the thing I've never been able to shake.

Your whole life, he told me, they teach you that all of it matters. You go to college, get the job, buy the house, marry, have the kids, and you work, and you work, and you work until you're finally old enough to stop. But by the time you get there, half your friends are gone. You retire. A couple years later your wife dies too. And now you're too old to enjoy any of it.

He'd reached the finish line everyone is running toward. And he was standing on it, telling me the prize wasn't there.

There is no "later" you're saving it for. You don't get the life back at the end.

I was twenty-two, and I'd never once been able to make that plan add up. He just handed me the words for why.

This is the law underneath everything I'm building now. You can win the whole game and still let it win you. You can do it all correctly, collect every prize they promised, and arrive at the end with the one thing you were actually here for already spent. The win means nothing if the running ate the years.

So define your enough. Draw the finish line yourself, closer than they told you, right around the life that's already good. Cross it on purpose. Then stop, and stay, and let that be the whole point.

They'll call it settling. Vovô wouldn't. He spent ninety-two years learning it the long way, then spent one evening trying to save me the trip. Come home to your life while you're still young enough to live in it.

There's more he taught me.

But some of it only lands once you've started walking home.
